Rail as a thin bus: ten scenarios

The thin bus (design A) in every situation a rail meets: one rail, several, on both sides, many taps, several end nodes, a different direction, a tap from the middle of a row, a rail inside a zone, labels, and a bus that feeds nodes instead of collecting. These are drawn by hand to settle the rules; nothing here is built yet.

The rules these pictures follow

  1. The bus is a light 2px line outside everything the diagram draws, on the side you name (default: right for a diagram flowing down, bottom for one flowing right).
  2. Each tap is a light dashed stub from the node straight to the bus, ending in a dot. No arrowhead on the stub; the arrowhead is on the bus line where it enters the end node (or at the nodes when the bus feeds them).
  3. One label for the rail, written once along the bus; if the taps carry different labels, each tap carries its own and the bus has none.
  4. The rail has a name (small, at the head of the bus), a tint when there are several (grey, blue, green…), and end nodes where the bus finishes.
  5. Several rails on one side run parallel, 70px apart; a tap to the farther rail hops over the nearer. Two sides are better than hops when the nodes allow it.
  6. A node with neighbours in the way leaves by the gap between rows and hops the links it crosses; nothing is routed over a node.

1 One rail, three taps

The base case. The bus runs beside the diagram on the side you name; each tap leaves its node sideways, ends in a dot; the end node sits where the bus finishes. Label once, along the bus.

2 Two rails on the same side

Parallel buses, 70px apart, each with its own tint and name at the top and its own end node. A tap to the farther bus hops over the nearer one. The nearer bus is the one with the fewer or shorter taps (fewest hops).

3 Rails on both sides

No hops, nothing crosses: each rail on its own side. The engine picks the side that puts the fewest nodes in the way, or you write [left] / [right]. Best way to carry two rails when the nodes allow it.

4 Many taps

Nine nodes on one bus: nine dots, nine light dashed stubs, one label. It stays readable because the stubs are all the same length and never cross. At about 20 taps the stubs could be dropped for a plain bar (design C) automatically.

5 A rail with two end nodes

The bus ends in a bracket that splits into one arrow per end node (SIEM and the archive). The bracket is as wide as the end nodes need; with one end node there is no bracket.

6 Direction right: the rail runs under the diagram

The same thing turned: a horizontal bus under the row, taps drop down from the nodes, the end node at the right end, the label above the line. The default side is bottom for a diagram that flows right, right for one that flows down.

7 A tap from a node in the middle of a row

Nodes on the edge of the diagram tap straight out. A node with neighbours between it and the rail (A1) leaves from its bottom, runs along the gap between the rows and hops over the vertical links to reach the bus. Hops are small arcs, never a gap in the line.

8 A rail inside a zone

`rail logs [in app]` : the bus and its end node live inside the zone, along its inner right edge. The rest of the diagram is untouched. Use it when a zone has its own logging and it should not look like part of the whole picture.

9 Taps that say different things

When the taps do not all carry the same label, there is no label on the bus: each tap carries its own, above its stub. When they share one it is written once on the bus (scenario 1).

10 A rail that feeds the nodes

The other direction: the end node is at the head of the bus and the arrowheads are at the nodes. The line is drawn the same; only which end carries the arrowheads changes, as with any link.
